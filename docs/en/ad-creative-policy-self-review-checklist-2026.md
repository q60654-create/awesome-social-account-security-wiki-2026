> **Key Takeaway:** Most ad rejections are process gaps, not bad luck. Fix a self-review checklist before every submission, write revisions and resubmissions as concrete actions, and approval rate becomes a metric you can track instead of a mood.

When a creative gets rejected repeatedly, do not rush to swap the asset, the account, or the submission time. In troubleshooting, the most common pattern is simple: the rejection notice already names the policy clause, but the team never checked that clause before submitting. This guide is written as executable actions. Read it, then embed the checklist into your creative launch process the same day.

## Where do high-frequency rejection causes actually cluster?

Start by tagging your historical rejection records by category. After two months you will see which risk points concentrate in your own account. Day-to-day rejections cluster in six groups:

1. **Exaggerated claims:** absolute language and outcome promises such as "best," "number one," or "guaranteed results."

2. **Before-and-after comparisons:** before/after imagery, or time-based promises like "results in days," which run high in health and beauty categories.

3. **Health sensitivity:** implied disease treatment, efficacy promises, or negative descriptions of personal physical traits.

4. **Financial sensitivity:** income promises, get-rich-quick implications, or loan-inducement wording.

5. **Targeting user traits:** directly or implicitly describing personal characteristics, or manufacturing appearance or age anxiety.

6. **Asset-category mismatch:** running category A creative for a category B product, where visuals and copy do not match.

These six often appear together. A single beauty asset may use a comparison image, state a time-to-result, and imply a skin problem. The rejection notice cites only one policy clause, but the revision must address several at once, otherwise the second submission gets stopped by another clause. So allow multi-select when tagging; do not record only the first reason.

## Why is copy-to-landing-page consistency the easiest thing to miss?

Review does not look only at the asset. It checks whether the asset, the landing page, and the product are self-consistent. The creative gets revised many times while the landing page still carries copy from six months ago — that is the weakest link in most teams, and the mismatch shows the moment the two are compared. The checks are specific:

- Any offer promised in the ad must be findable on the landing page with identical terms and wording;

- If the ad says free trial, the landing page must not contain hidden subscriptions or pre-checked billing items;

- The landing page must carry complete business information: brand name, contact method, return and cancellation policies, and the page must load normally without obvious dead links;

- Do not use celebrity images or media screenshots unrelated to the business as endorsements, and do not repurpose third-party brand elements.

The trap is that this is not something the creative team can catch alone. Creative is produced by media buying or design; the landing page is maintained by operations or the site builder. With no shared checkpoint, the drift persists. The practice that works: set one comparison check before launch, where the same person opens the creative preview and the landing page side by side and compares item by item, instead of each side confirming only its own part.

## How do you put the pre-submission checklist into the workflow?

The checklist itself is not complex. What matters is fixing it in the process rather than relying on memory. Run every item before each creative goes live:

1. Copy contains no absolute language and no efficacy or income promises;

2. Images contain no before/after comparison, no close-ups of physical defects, no excessive retouching;

3. Landing page matches the creative offer, and policy pages are reachable and in the matching language;

4. Domain is verified, the page has no forced redirects and no autoplay audio;

5. Target-market language matches landing-page language, with no obvious machine-translation artifacts;

6. The creative uses no unauthorized third-party IP or copyrighted material;

7. Creative style stays continuous with the account's historical delivery, avoiding a sudden style shift that triggers manual review.

Implementation can be lightweight: turn it into a pre-submission checkbox form, where whoever submits ticks the boxes, and the tick record is archived with the creative version. When a rejection arrives, you can immediately trace which item was missed instead of guessing from scratch. The value of a checklist is not the number of items; it is using the same standard every time so that different submitters share a consistent floor.

## After a rejection, how should you revise and resubmit?

When a rejection arrives, read the specific policy clause cited in the notice first, then act. Do not make sweeping changes on instinct. A reusable action order:

1. Change only the elements suspected of violation; do not do a large-scale rebuild, and keep the original version for comparison;

2. Record the version number and change points after editing, track which version passed, and build a team case library;

3. After resubmitting, do not edit repeatedly right away — every edit re-enters the review queue and resets the clock for nothing;

4. If the same creative is rejected twice, switch creative direction outright instead of tweaking a few words to force it through;

5. If you are confident it is compliant and it is still rejected, use the appeal channel, attach a revision comparison and compliance basis, keep the tone restrained, and state only facts.

The third item is the most overlooked. Many teams cannot resist editing one more version while waiting for review, and each edit puts the creative back in the queue, lengthening the wait and destroying the mapping between "which version corresponds to which review result." Write "do not touch after submission" as an explicit rule; it is more effective than explaining afterward why the wait was so long.

## How does self-review strategy differ by account state?

Account state affects how you absorb risk; it does not change the review policy itself. The table below compares common dimensions to help decide where to concentrate self-review resources:

| Dimension | Account with delivery guarantee | Account carrying its own risk | Self-review difference |

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Cost of rejection | There is a backstop; direct loss per rejection is relatively contained | Loss is borne internally; each rejection costs more | The latter needs one extra review pass before submission |

| Self-review strictness | Run the checklist routinely, focus on the six high-frequency groups | Two-person item-by-item review, especially landing-page consistency | The latter adds a cross-signature |

| Resubmission rhythm | Small changes to validate first, then decide whether to switch direction | Prioritize getting it right in one pass to reduce queue churn | The latter consolidates changes into one round |

| Retrospective focus | Look at rejection category distribution to optimize creative direction | Look at process gaps and fill pre-submission checkpoints | Both share the same case library |

Regardless of account type, exaggerated claims, before/after comparisons, and health and financial sensitivity are never relaxed by account type, and checklist items should not be trimmed because of it. ## Why does the same cause get rejected over and over?

Rejection itself is not frightening. The frightening part is the same cause recurring, which means the retrospective never made it into the SOP. To judge whether a retrospective worked, watch three signals: are rejection records tagged under a unified taxonomy; is the interval between same-category rejections lengthening; and does new creative pass checklist verification before submission. If none of the three improves, the retrospective stopped at verbal discussion and never became a concrete pre-submission action.

One more commonly missed point: creative compliance and account environment are two separate lines. The creative may be compliant, but an abnormal login environment or frequent device switching will still stall the delivery action. Handle that class of problem in the order given by the [post-delivery account security self-check](https://facai.lol/blog/telegram-account-delivery-security-checklist): stabilize the login state first, then talk about creative iteration. ## How do you run a reproducible self-review test?

Here is a minimal test you can run today to verify whether your checklist actually intercepts problems:

1. Pick five items from historical rejections, hide the rejection reasons, and keep only the creative and landing page;

2. Have two colleagues each run the checklist item by item and independently write down a "would reject / would not reject" judgment;

3. Compare against the real outcomes and count which checklist items the misses cluster on;

4. Move the two most-missed items to the top of the checklist and assign a reviewer;

5. Rerun the same sample two weeks later and see whether misses decline.

This test depends on no external tool. It verifies one thing: whether the checklist produces the same result when used by different people. If two people diverge sharply on the same creative, the problem is ambiguous checklist wording, not the people executing it.

## FAQ and after-sales troubleshooting

### Q: The creative was rejected the first time. Should I rebuild it immediately?

Not recommended. Read the specific policy clause cited in the rejection notice first, change only the suspected elements, and keep the original version for comparison. A large rebuild leaves you unable to tell which change produced the approval, and wastes the localization information this rejection just gave you.

### Q: Can I keep editing the creative after resubmitting?

Do not edit repeatedly right away. Every edit puts the creative back into the review queue, which resets the waiting clock and scrambles the mapping between versions and review results. Wait for the result after submission and consolidate changes into the next round.

### Q: The same creative was rejected twice. Is tweaking the copy workable?

No. Two rejections usually mean the problem is not individual wording but the creative direction itself. Switch direction outright rather than tweaking a few words to force it through, which only burns submission attempts and waiting time.

### Q: The creative is compliant but still rejected. What now?

Use the appeal channel. Attach a revision comparison and compliance basis, state that each policy clause was checked item by item, keep the tone restrained, and state only facts. Record the case in the team case library for future reference on similar creative.

### Q: Can an abnormal account login state affect creative delivery decisions?

Yes. Creative compliance and account environment are two separate lines. When the login state is unstable, handle it in the order of the account security self-check first, then return to creative iteration. For delivery issues, problems appearing within 24 hours of first login are handled under the replacement guarantee; beyond that window, or after a password change, rebinding, resale, or policy-violating use, the guarantee does not apply. Facebook durable accounts carry a 90-day quality guarantee covering non-human login failures and passive bans; password changes, rebinding, violating content, and automation-tool issues are outside the scope. For appeals, provide the order number, first-login time, error screenshots, and a note on whether the password was changed.

Embed the self-review checklist into the launch workflow and rejection rate becomes a manageable variable. The checklist handles pre-submission interception, the resubmission process handles post-rejection convergence, and the case library turns single experiences into team assets. Together, these three form a creative compliance process that can run long term.
