> **Key Takeaway:** When an ad account is restricted, classify the restriction type before you appeal, submit a complete evidence pack in one pass, keep the appeal factual and short, and hold all sensitive account actions during the wait. After reinstatement, ramp spend gradually for two weeks. Most recoverable accounts come back this way.

A restriction notice is a routine event for any team running paid social traffic, but a restriction is not the same as a dead asset. What decides the outcome is what you do in the first few hours: whether you classify the restriction correctly, whether the evidence pack is complete on first submission, whether the appeal reads as a statement rather than an argument, and whether you leave the account alone while it is under review. Get those four right and most recoverable accounts return to normal operation. Get them wrong and a fixable account turns into a write-off. This guide breaks the workflow into four stages — classify, assemble, appeal, de-sensitize — and gives an executable action for each.

## What should you do first when a restriction notice arrives?

Do not click appeal yet. Read the notice twice and determine which category of restriction you are dealing with. A misclassified restriction sends the appeal in the wrong direction and burns both appeal attempts and platform trust. Most restrictions fall into three categories:

1. **Policy violation.** The notice names a specific ad, creative, landing page, or product category. This is resolved by remediation, not by argument.

2. **Risk-control false positive.** The wording is usually "unusual activity" or "suspicious behavior" and does not name a specific ad. Common triggers are a new account requesting a rapid spend-limit increase, a sudden change in login environment, an abnormal spend curve, or a burst of edits in a short window.

3. **Payment problem.** A charge failed, the card issuer declined, or there is a billing dispute. Fix the billing issue first; no appeal wording will help until the payment state is clean.

When you are unsure, apply one dividing line: **does the notice mention a specific ad?** If it does, take the policy remediation path. If it does not, check login environment, operating cadence, and payment status first, then decide what evidence to prepare. This single step determines the direction of everything that follows, so make it a fixed item on your team checklist rather than an improvised judgment call.

## How do you assemble the appeal evidence pack in one pass?

Submit once, with everything included. Piecemeal supplements stretch the review cycle and make it harder for the reviewer to form a complete picture. Prepare four groups of material:

- **Asset-chain description:** the business management platform name, account ID, linked pages, domains, and pixel ownership — enough to show the asset structure is internally consistent.

- **Business authenticity material:** business license, a description of the service or product, and an overview of historical delivery (countries, categories, rough volume).

- **Remediation record (policy violation cases):** screenshots of removed or edited creatives, and before/after comparisons of the landing page.

- **Environment explanation (risk-control false positive cases):** the login environment and the mapping between operators and accounts, explaining the cause of the behavior change — an office move, a device change, a proxy switch.

The materials must corroborate each other: the registered entity, the business shown on the page, and the product sold on the landing page must tell the same story. Any mismatch invites doubt about overall authenticity and lengthens the review. The first thing to do after recovery is rebuild your backups; for a concrete checklist see [Facebook account backup strategy](https://facai.lol/blog/fb-account-backup-checkpoint-recovery-2026), so you are not caught without records the next time a restriction lands.

## How do you write an appeal that does not backfire?

State facts only, in three parts: what happened, what has been remediated, and what you are asking to be reviewed. Keep it under half a page. Do not write emotionally, do not speculate about how the platform's systems work, and do not pile on historical results unrelated to this restriction. A restrained tone is itself part of your credibility — the reviewer should read a statement, not a defense argument.

After submission you enter the waiting period. Many accounts are lost not to the appeal itself but to what happens while waiting:

- Stop all sensitive operations inside the business management platform that holds the restricted account: no new accounts, no payment method changes, no asset migration, no page changes.

- Deliberately slow down other healthy accounts in the same environment: lower spend, reduce new ad creation and edit frequency, so you do not trigger a chain reaction.

- Do not move rejected creatives unchanged into another account and rerun them. That is how a single-point problem becomes a matrix-wide problem.

- Assign one person to track the appeal status. All replies go out under one account voice, so you do not create conflicting information through multiple channels.

## What asset-protection actions belong in the waiting period?

The core principle of the waiting period is "do not fiddle." The fewer sensitive operations, the higher the account's recoverability. The checklist below can go straight into your team's operating manual; tick each item every time a restriction occurs:

| Check item | Restricted account | Other accounts in same environment | Pass criterion |

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Create new ad accounts | Prohibited | Paused | Zero new accounts during the wait |

| Change payment method | Prohibited | Paused | No changes until the appeal concludes |

| Asset migration and page changes | Prohibited | Paused | No cross-platform asset moves |

| Spend cadence | Stopped | Actively reduced | Run one notch below normal |

| Creative reuse | No unchanged reruns | No unchanged reruns | Edit creative before relaunching |

| Communication voice | One designated responder | Same voice | No multiple parallel replies |

The purpose of this table is to remove on-the-spot debate. The most common reflex after a restriction is "let's just spin up another account and keep running," and that reflex is precisely the usual path from a single-point problem to a matrix-wide one.

## Why does de-sensitizing matter after the account is restored?

Receiving the reinstatement notice is not the finish line. The first two weeks carry elevated risk of a second review. Right after a restriction is lifted, any sharp change can be re-read as anomalous, so cadence discipline in the recovery period matters even more than during the appeal:

1. Start with small-budget, mature campaigns and ramp spend gradually. Do not jump straight back to pre-restriction levels.

2. For the first two weeks, do not change the payment method, do not restructure the account, and do not bulk-duplicate campaigns.

3. Keep the login environment identical to what you used during the appeal. Do not swap proxies or devices on a whim.

4. Archive the reinstatement notice and the appeal record as a team asset file, so the next similar event can reuse it directly.

Restructuring aggressively and pushing spend hard on the day of reinstatement is an easy way to send a freshly returned account back into the review queue. Treat these four steps as standard procedure and the probability of a second restriction drops noticeably.

## How does appeal handling differ between accounts with and without a replacement guarantee?

Teams sourcing account resources commonly encounter two commercial framings: accounts covered by a replacement guarantee and accounts without one. The difference in appeal and asset handling is set out below. Specific terms depend on the account state and the actual agreement; this table compares process only.

| Dimension | With replacement guarantee | Without replacement guarantee |

| --- | --- | --- |

| Focus after restriction | Report through the agreed process first, then push the appeal in parallel | The team bears the appeal and recovery entirely |

| Evidence responsibility | Usually requires cooperating with asset-chain and business material | The team assembles all material itself |

| Appeal attempt management | Submit on the agreed cadence to avoid invalid appeals | The team decides its own appeal cadence |

| Recovery-period operation | Still requires de-sensitizing so a second restriction does not affect the determination | De-sensitizing is equally necessary; risk is self-borne |

| Record archiving | Reporting and appeal records must be kept complete | Archiving is recommended for later review |

Whichever framing applies, the principle "do not fiddle during the appeal" is identical. Pasting this table into the operating manual removes a lot of improvised argument. ## How do you turn appeals into a reusable team process?

A single recovery is luck; long-term survival is process. Treat every restriction as a process review: record the restriction type, the notice wording, the evidence list submitted, the number of appeal rounds, and the outcome, and build a searchable archive. When a similar notice arrives next time, pull the historical template directly and preparation time drops sharply.

At the same time, fix the responsible person for each of the four stages — classify, assemble, appeal, de-sensitize — so communication does not fragment. Assigning one person to track status and unify the reply voice is the step most teams skip and the one that matters most. ## FAQ and after-sales troubleshooting

### Q: What is the first thing to do after receiving a restriction notice?

Do not click appeal. Read the notice wording and classify it: does it name a specific ad or policy clause? If it names a specific ad, take the policy remediation path. If it does not, check login environment, operating cadence, and payment status first, then decide the evidence direction.

### Q: What if the appeal is submitted and a supplement is requested?

Respond item by item to the supplement request. Do not dump unrelated material in at once. Keep the supplement consistent with the original submission — entity, page, and landing page information must match. Reply under one account voice to avoid conflicting information from multiple channels.

### Q: Should other healthy accounts stop delivery during the appeal?

Not necessarily all of them, but slow them down deliberately: lower spend, reduce new ad creation and edit frequency, and pause payment-method changes and asset migration. The goal is to prevent abnormal operations in the same environment from stacking up and turning a single restriction into a matrix-wide problem.

### Q: Can I increase spend immediately after the account is restored?

Not advisable. The first two weeks carry elevated second-review risk. Start with small-budget mature campaigns, ramp spend gradually, do not change the payment method, do not restructure the account, do not bulk-duplicate campaigns, and keep the login environment identical to the appeal period.

### Q: A delivered account is restricted within 24 hours of first login — how do I report it?

Handle it under the first-login 24-hour replacement guarantee: the clock starts at the buyer's first successful login. Within 24 hours, inability to log in, a changed password, a ban on delivery, or a clear mismatch with the product description qualifies for the guarantee process. Provide the order number, first-login time, error screenshots, and a statement of whether the password was changed. Risk-control events caused by changing the password, rebinding, resale, or use of bulk-control tooling fall outside the guarantee.

### Q: A Facebook durable account is restricted inside the guarantee window — is it covered?

Facebook durable accounts carry a 90-day quality guarantee covering non-human login failures and passive bans, with screenshots and timestamps required. Not covered: changed password, rebound email, changed name or profile, bulk login of multiple accounts on one device, use of automation tooling, posting violating content, a failed self-appeal, or self-deactivation. Beyond the window, only troubleshooting advice is provided. Specific terms depend on the account state and the live page description.
